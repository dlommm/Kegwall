Kegwall V2, the design

The full V2 redesign, approved for planning. It covers 14 wall looks, one admin design across every page, and the pour takeovers, including the live Pour Lab. Everything is rendered from working prototypes with a synthetic taproom. Nothing in the app has changed yet.

Decided

  1. One visual language for the whole product: the beer’s own color as data, tap markers, pints left as the unit, and status written as sentences. Big Shoulders Display and Atkinson Hyperlegible.
  2. 14 wall looks. The 11 existing IDs are each redrawn from their name, plus Just Tapped, Tap Handles and Tally. Every look shows what’s left as a picture and in pints.
  3. One admin design replaces the three appearances: six destinations, one page template, and every current feature kept.
  4. Pour takeovers each have a signature effect and a real beer stream, and they adapt live to however many taps pour at once. Wall moments: Just kicked, Just tapped, Milestone.
  5. Honesty and performance: measured volumes only, with the recorded volume on the finish. Motion is compositor-only so it suits a Pi, there’s a reduced-motion fallback, and stored configs stay compatible.

Why the last round read as generated

  • One skeleton, three skins. Header, eight equal rounded boxes, footer. The "list" and "columns" concepts were the same grid turned sideways.
  • Nothing led. Every tap had the same weight, so the eye had nowhere to land from across the room.
  • Icons standing in for ideas. A small SVG glass on every card was decoration, not information.
  • The default palettes. Dark ground, cream type, a copper accent, plus a paper-and-serif "editorial" option. Both are where generated pages drift.
  • Status as tiles. Levels as percent bars and badges, where a bartender would just say "two pints left".
  • Today's app shares the problem. The classic wall reads as color swatches, and the dashboard is a sidebar, a list of equal alerts and a row of tiles.

A · Just TappedRecommended base

A bar's "just tapped" board: the newest keg gets half the screen as a field of its own color, and when a tap pours, that half becomes the glass.

What it gets right

  • Reads as a taproom in one second, and the beer leads instead of the chrome.
  • The pour is the same composition, not an overlay, so there's nothing jarring and it costs little on a Pi.
  • "Pints left" is real Kegwall data in a bartender's words.
  • Works with zero artwork: every beer gets a tap marker made from its color and initials, and uploaded label art can replace it.

Risks

  • It needs a rule for what gets the spotlight (newest keg by default, or a beer the owner pins).
  • Very pale and very dark beers need computed ink and tone adjustments. The prototype already does this, but it needs a contrast test.
  • The list holds about 11 rows. More taps paginate.

B · Tap Handles

The bar back itself: every tap is a handle in its beer's color with a sight glass for the keg, and the pouring handle tips forward like a pulled tap.

What it gets right

  • The most physical and memorable: the wall looks like the bar.
  • A pour is visible from anywhere in the room, without a takeover.
  • Eight beer colors side by side make the wall itself the decoration.

Risks

  • Eight handles per page at most, so 12 taps always paginate.
  • Long names have to shrink, and there's no room for descriptions.
  • The easiest of the three to tip into kitsch. Better as one wall look than as the whole product.

C · Tally

A bartender's tally board: the night is written as a sentence, and every keg is its remaining pints as tally marks.

What it gets right

  • The tally is new data design: the pint is the unit, and an emptying keg is something you can see.
  • Calm and high-contrast. The best for daylight rooms and small TVs.
  • Scales to any tap count, and the sentence carries the news.

Risks

  • A bright screen in a dark bar at night glares, so it needs a night variant.
  • A half-barrel is 124 pints, which needs a rule such as "one mark = 5 pints".
  • The least emotional of the three. It informs more than it sells.

Fourteen looks, every one redrawn from its name

The last round only re-skinned the old looks. This time each one starts again from what its name means, keeping its ID so saved walls still work. Every look follows one rule: it shows what's left twice, as a picture you can read across the room (a fill, gauge, plate or tally) and as pints left.

One admin, every feature

Midnight, Workshop and Console become a single design. Every page uses the same template, so nothing feels like a different app:

  • A sentence that says what needs doing, one line of support, and at most one main button.
  • Tabs for that area's sections, one main list or table, and details or editing in a drawer on the right.
  • Six destinations in the top bar, with notifications and settings at the right.

Pour takeovers

The moment the app is known for. Open the live Pour Lab to watch each one play a whole pour, switch between 1, 2 or 3 pours at once, and try the Cheers finish and reduced motion. The counter only ever shows measured ounces, and the finish shows the recorded volume.

Next: the implementation plan

The blueprint in the repo (docs/design/kegwall-v2/UI_REDESIGN_BLUEPRINT.md, v0.8) holds every decision, the architecture record (tokens and CSS Modules, admin-only accessible primitives, a 1920×1080 TV stage), the compatibility rules for stored walls, and the ten milestones. The next step is a detailed plan for each milestone: files, tests, screenshots to compare, and how to roll back. No production code changes until you approve that plan.

All beers, levels, pours and people are synthetic. These are proposals rendered from standalone prototypes; nothing in the app has changed. Directions A, B and C above are how we got here; A’s language became the product, and B and C became looks.
